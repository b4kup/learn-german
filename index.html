A complete course · A1 to C2

German Grammar for Danish Speakers

Tysk grammatik for dansktalende — fra begynder til avanceret

A full, level-tagged German grammar course written in English for a native Danish speaker. Every chapter exploits the fact that Danish and German are both V2 Germanic languages with shared vocabulary, while systematically drilling the three things Danish speakers get wrong most: the four-case system, three grammatical genders (vs. Danish two), and adjective declension.

How to use this course

Methodology · read this first

Why this order?

German grammar is a system where later rules depend on earlier ones. You cannot do adjective endings (Ch. 18) without cases (Ch. 9–14); you cannot do cases without gender and articles (Ch. 2–4). The spine of the course is: sounds → nouns, gender, articles → pronouns and present tense → word order → the cases, one at a time → everything that depends on cases (prepositions, possessives, adjectives) → the tense system → complex sentences → advanced and stylistic grammar.

The design follows second-language-acquisition research: explicit grammar anchored in meaningful examples (Long’s Focus on Form), graded examples that recycle known material (comprehensible-input coverage research by Laufer, Hu & Nation, Schmitt), quizzes as retrieval practice (Roediger & Karpicke found delayed recall of 61% for a tested group vs. 40% for a re-study group), and deliberate recycling of earlier grammar in later chapters (the spacing effect).

How the Danish parallels help

Wherever German works like Danish, the course says so explicitly and you can move fast (positive transfer). Wherever German differs, a Danish trap box flags it so you do not transfer a Danish habit that produces a German error (negative transfer, in the sense of Lado’s contrastive analysis). Danish terms used: fælleskøn / intetkøn (common / neuter gender), ledsætning (subordinate clause), helsætning (main clause), bøjning (inflection).

Quiz and exercise routine

After reading a chapter, do the quiz (recognition) first, then the exercise (production). Check answers only after finishing — they are hidden behind a button in each block. Then re-do the exercise from two chapters back; this spaced retrieval roughly halves your forgetting. Aim to understand 95–98% of every example before moving on.

Level tags

The A1–C2 tags show the level at which each structure is normally introduced, so you can skip or prioritise. As an A2-to-B1 learner: Chapters 1–8 are revision, 9–23 are your core work, 24–30 are your roadmap.

1 · Pronunciation & spelling basics A1

Sounds, special letters, and the Danish sound bridge

German spelling is highly phonetic — good news for Danes, who are used to a much less phonetic orthography. Key points:

  • Special letters: ä, ö, ü (Umlaut) and ß (Eszett). Note that German ä ≈ Danish e/æ, ö ≈ Danish ø, ü ≈ Danish y. Strong positive transfer: a Dane can already pronounce ü (like ny) and ö (like øl) — sounds that torment English speakers.
  • ß = “ss” after a long vowel or diphthong: Straße (street), heiß (hot). Written “ss” after short vowels: muss.
  • sch = English “sh” (Schule, cf. Danish skole). Initial st- / sp- are pronounced “sht- / shp-” (Stein, Sprache).
  • w is pronounced like Danish “v” (Wasser); German v is usually “f” (Vater); German z is “ts” (Zeit).
  • ch has two sounds: hard after a/o/u (Bach), soft after e/i (ich).
Dansk parallel · the sound bridge

German underwent the High German consonant shift; Danish did not. So Danish cognates predict German spellings. Use this table constantly:

2 · Nouns & grammatical gender A1

Three genders where Danish has two — the single biggest structural difference

German nouns are always capitalised (das Haus, der Mann, die Frau) — Danish does not do this. And German has three genders, where Danish has two:

DanishGerman
“common” genderfælleskøn (en-ord): en mand, en bog — about 75% of all Danish nounssplits into masculine der and feminine die
neuterintetkøn (et-ord): et hus, et barn — about 24%neuter das
Danish trap

Danish fælleskøn corresponds to both German der and die, and you cannot predict which from Danish. Learn every German noun together with its article (learn das Haus, not Haus). This is the number-one piece of advice for a Danish learner.

Gender signposts (helpful, not absolute)

  • Usually masculine (der): male people; days, months, seasons (der Montag); most nouns ending -er, -ling, -ismus.
  • Usually feminine (die): female people; endings -ung, -heit, -keit, -schaft, -ion, -tät and many in -e.
  • Usually neuter (das): young beings (das Kind, das Baby); diminutives -chen, -lein; endings -um, -ment; infinitives used as nouns.
Dansk parallel

Danish -hed → German -heit (frihed / Freiheit), both feminine; Danish -ing/-ning → German -ung (regering / Regierung), both feminine. Positive transfer.

3 · Definite & indefinite articles (nominative) A1

der / die / das and ein / eine — and why German never says “huset”

German articles carry gender, number and case information — Danish articles mostly do not inflect for case at all, so this is where German gets heavier.

masculinefeminineneuterplural
definite (“the”)derdiedasdie
indefinite (“a/an”)eineineein—
Danish trap · no article suffix

Danish attaches the definite article as a suffix: hus → huset, mand → manden. German never does this — it always uses a separate word in front: das Haus, der Mann. German is closer to the Danish construction used with an adjective (det store hus), where Danish also puts a free-standing den/det/de in front. So: German = always “det store hus” style, never “huset” style.

Dansk parallel

German indefinite ein / eine ≈ Danish en / et, and even sounds related: ein Mann / en mand; ein Haus / et hus.

4 · Personal pronouns & the present tense A1

Endings for every person — a habit Danish never taught you

Personal pronouns (nominative): ich (I), du (you, informal sg), er / sie / es (he / she / it), wir (we), ihr (you, informal pl), sie (they), Sie (you, formal).

Present-tense regular conjugation — endings on the stem (machen “to do/make”, cf. Danish at gøre):

PersonEndingExample
ich-emache
du-stmachst
er / sie / es-tmacht
wir-enmachen
ihr-tmacht
sie / Sie-enmachen
Danish trap · German conjugates for person

Danish uses one form for all persons: jeg gør, du gør, han gør, vi gør. German changes the ending every time (ich mache, du machst, er macht). You must actively re-learn the habit of adding personal endings. On the plus side, German has only one present tense — no separate progressive; ich mache covers both “I make” and “I am making”, just as Danish jeg gør does.

Dansk parallel · du vs. Sie

German distinguishes informal du (family, friends, children) from formal Sie (strangers, officials, work). Modern Danish has largely abandoned formal De in favour of universal du, so you must consciously re-activate the distinction Danish dropped. When in doubt in Germany: use Sie with adults you do not know.

6 · Word order basics: the V2 rule A1

Your biggest Danish head start

German main clauses (Hauptsätze) obey the verb-second (V2) rule: the finite verb is the second element, and exactly one constituent precedes it. Danish has the identical rule.

  • Ich trinke heute Kaffee. / Jeg drikker kaffe i dag. (I drink coffee today.)
  • Front something else, and subject and verb invert — in both languages: Heute trinke ich Kaffee. / I dag drikker jeg kaffe.
Dansk parallel · trust your instinct

The classic English-interference error Heute ich trinke… is one a Dane is unlikely to make, because Danish forbids it too (I dag jeg drikker is wrong in Danish). Trust your Danish instinct here.

The finite verb goes second; any non-finite parts (infinitives, participles, separable prefixes) go to the end, forming the Satzklammer (“sentence bracket”). Danish does the same: jeg vil gerne spise → ich möchte gern essen.

7 · Questions A1

8 · Negation: nicht & kein A1

Splitting Danish ikke into two German words
  • kein negates a noun that has an indefinite article or no article (“not a / no”): Ich habe kein Auto. (I have no car.) kein declines like ein.
  • nicht negates everything else — verbs, adjectives, adverbs, or a noun with a definite article: Ich arbeite nicht. · Das ist nicht mein Auto.
Danish trap · one ikke, two German negators

Danish uses one word, ikke, for nearly all negation (jeg har ikke en bil, jeg arbejder ikke). You must split this habit into German kein (before indefinite or bare nouns) vs. nicht (elsewhere). Placement of nicht is Danish-friendly, though: like ikke, it generally comes after the finite verb and its objects: Ich kenne ihn nicht / Jeg kender ham ikke.

9 · The nominative case (Nominativ) A1–A2

The subject case — and why cases exist at all

The Nominativ is the subject case — the “doer” and the dictionary form. You have already been using it. It answers wer / was? (who / what?). It is also used for the predicate noun after sein, werden, bleiben: Er ist ein guter Lehrer.

German has four cases. The traditional German order is Nominativ, Genitiv, Dativ, Akkusativ; the common teaching order — used here — is Nominativ, Akkusativ, Dativ, Genitiv, matching everyday frequency.

Danish trap · the central challenge

The whole case system is the central German challenge for Danes: Old Norse had cases, but modern Danish has essentially lost them on nouns and articles. Only some pronouns keep them: jeg / mig, han / ham — exactly like English I / me, he / him. Use those surviving Danish pairs as your bridge: han (Nom) vs. ham (Akk/Dat) is the same idea as er vs. ihn / ihm.

10 · The accusative case (Akkusativ) A2

Direct objects — only the masculine changes

The Akkusativ marks the direct object — it answers wen / was? The good news: only the masculine article changes; feminine, neuter and plural look identical to the nominative.

NominativAkkusativ
masculineder / einden / einen
femininedie / einedie / eine
neuterdas / eindas / ein
pluraldiedie

Der Mann kauft den Wagen. (The man buys the car.) · Ich sehe einen Hund.

Danish trap · word order no longer carries the meaning

In the pair Der Hund beißt den Mann vs. Den Mann beißt der Hund, both mean “the dog bites the man”, because den marks the man as the object regardless of position. Danish (like English) relies on word order for this; German relies on case. Learn to read the article endings, not just the order.

The accusative also follows certain prepositions (Ch. 15) and time expressions (jeden Tag = every day).

11 · The dative case (Dativ) A2

The recipient case — all genders change

The Dativ marks the indirect object — the recipient — and answers wem? (to whom?). All genders change:

NominativDativ
masculineder / eindem / einem
femininedie / eineder / einer
neuterdas / eindem / einem
pluraldieden (+ -n on the noun)

Ich gebe dem Mann das Buch. (I give the man the book.) · Sie hilft der Frau.

Dansk parallel · the “giving / to” case

Danish expresses the recipient through word order or til: Jeg giver manden bogen / Jeg giver bogen til manden. In German, the dem ending does that work. Mnemonic: the dative endings -m and -r echo English him / her — and Danish ham / hende shows the same object logic.

Common verbs that take the dative: helfen (help), danken (thank), gehören (belong to), gefallen (please / like).

Danish trap

Dative plural adds -n to the noun: mit den Kindern, mit den Freunden. Danish has no such rule.

13 · Possessives & the genitive case (Genitiv) A2–B1

mein / dein / sein — and mandens bil vs. der Wagen des Mannes

Possessive articles (“ein-words”) take the same endings as ein / kein: mein (my), dein (your), sein (his/its), ihr (her/their), unser (our), euer (your pl), Ihr (your formal). Das ist mein Bruder. · Ich sehe meinen Bruder (Akk).

The genitive shows possession — it answers wessen? (whose?). Masculine and neuter articles become des (+ -s / -es on the noun); feminine and plural become der: das Auto des Mannes, das Haus der Frau.

Dansk parallel

Danish forms the genitive with a simple -s (like English): mandens bil. German also uses an -s on masculine/neuter nouns but additionally changes the article to des / der and typically puts the possessor after the noun: der Wagen des Mannes. In speech, Germans often avoid the genitive with von + Dativ (der Wagen von dem Mann) — structurally like Danish bilen til manden and a useful fallback.

15 · Prepositions by case A2–B1

Fixed-case prepositions and the nine two-way Wechselpräpositionen

German prepositions govern a fixed case — new for Danes, whose prepositions never trigger case changes.

  • Always Akkusativ: durch, für, gegen, ohne, um (mnemonic “DFGOU”).
  • Always Dativ: aus, bei, mit, nach, seit, von, zu (and gegenüber).
  • Two-way (Wechselpräpositionen), nine of them: an, auf, hinter, in, neben, über, unter, vor, zwischen. These take Akkusativ for movement / direction (wohin?) and Dativ for location (wo?).

Ich gehe in die Schule. (into — Akk, movement) vs. Ich bin in der Schule. (in — Dat, location)
Ich hänge das Bild an die Wand (Akk) vs. Das Bild hängt an der Wand (Dat).

Dansk parallel · the concept is familiar

Danish makes the same semantic distinction with different words — ind i (into) vs. i (in), hen på vs. på — but never changes the article. The concept (movement vs. location) is intuitive for a Dane; the mechanism (switching Akk/Dat) is what is new.

Common contractions: in+das=ins, in+dem=im, an+das=ans, an+dem=am, zu+dem=zum, zu+der=zur.

17 · Past tenses: Perfekt & Präteritum A2–B1

Structurally identical to Danish perfektum — but a new stylistic split

Perfekt (spoken past) = auxiliary haben / sein (present) + past participle (Partizip II) at the end. Structurally identical to Danish perfektum with har / er:

  • Ich habe Fußball gespielt = Jeg har spillet fodbold.
  • sein-verbs: verbs of motion or change of state use sein, exactly like Danish uses er / være: Ich bin nach Berlin gefahren = Jeg er kørt til Berlin. The Danish har / er split maps almost one-to-one onto haben / sein — a major positive transfer.
  • Participle formation: regular ge-…-t (gespielt), strong ge-…-en with vowel change (gesungen). Inseparable and -ieren verbs take no ge- (verstanden, studiert).

Präteritum (simple / written past): ich spielte, ich ging. In everyday spoken German it is mostly reserved for sein (war), haben (hatte), and the modals (konnte, wollte…); narratives and writing use it fully.

Danish trap · a stylistic split, not a formal one

Danish uses its simple past (spillede, gik) evenly across speech and writing. German splits by register: Perfekt in speech, Präteritum in writing. The forms are easy for you; the habit of switching by register is what to learn.

18 · Adjective declension B1

The second great Danish challenge, alongside cases

In German, an adjective before a noun takes an ending that depends on gender + case + article type. Three patterns:

  • Weak (after definite der/die/das): endings are just -e or -en. der alte Mann, die alte Frau, den alten Mann.
  • Mixed (after ein / kein / possessives): ein alter Mann, ein altes Haus, eine alte Frau.
  • Strong (no article): the adjective carries the full marker. kalter Kaffee, kalte Milch, kaltes Wasser.
The master principle

The gender / case signal must appear exactly once — on the article or the adjective. If the article shows it (der), the adjective relaxes to -e/-en; if not, the adjective takes it over. Learn weak → mixed → strong, in that order.

Dansk parallel · familiar idea, extra dimension

Danish does inflect adjectives, but only for gender / number / definiteness — never for case: en stor bil, et stort hus, store biler; den store bil. So the idea of adjective agreement is familiar, but German adds the case dimension and a three-way article-based system. Do not assume stor / stort / store maps cleanly — German has far more endings.

22 · Subordinate clauses & conjunctions B1

Verb-final Nebensätze — a trap Danes get half-right

In German subordinate clauses (Nebensätze) introduced by a subordinating conjunction, the finite verb goes to the very end: Ich bleibe zu Hause, weil ich krank bin. (I stay home because I am ill.)

Danish trap · closer than English, but not identical

Danish subordinate clauses (ledsætninger, after at, fordi, hvis, når) also abandon V2 and use a special order — but Danish keeps Subject–Adverb–Verb (fordi jeg ikke er syg): the verb stays relatively early, only after the sentence adverb. German pushes the verb all the way to the end, after all objects and complements: weil ich gestern sehr krank war. You already have the instinct that subordinate clauses are different (unlike English speakers) — the job is to extend it to full verb-final placement.

  • Coordinating conjunctions (und, aber, oder, denn, sondern) do not change word order — same as Danish og, men, eller.
  • Subordinating conjunctions (weil, dass, wenn, als, ob, obwohl, damit, während) trigger verb-final order.
  • If the subordinate clause comes first, the whole clause counts as position 1, so the main-clause verb follows the comma directly: Weil ich krank bin, bleibe ich zu Hause. Danish does the same inversion: Fordi jeg er syg, bliver jeg hjemme.

23 · Relative clauses B1–B2

Inflected relative pronouns — unlike Danish der / som

Relative clauses describe a noun and are introduced by a relative pronoun identical in form to the definite article (der, die, das, den, dem…), agreeing in gender and number with the antecedent but taking its case from its role inside the relative clause. The verb goes to the end (it is a subordinate clause).

  • Der Mann, der dort steht, ist mein Vater. (subject → Nom der)
  • Der Mann, den ich sehe, … (object → Akk den)
  • Der Mann, dem ich helfe, … (dative)
Danish trap · two new habits

Danish uses uninflected der (for subjects) and som (subject or object, often droppable): Manden, der står der / Manden, som jeg ser. German relative pronouns inflect for case and can never be dropped. You must (a) stop dropping the pronoun and (b) start choosing its case.

25 · Konjunktiv II (conditional / unreal) B2

Hypotheticals, wishes, and politeness — würde ≈ ville

Used for hypotheticals, wishes, and politeness. Two ways to form it:

28 · Konjunktiv I & reported speech C1

A mood Danish simply does not have

Konjunktiv I marks reported speech neutrally (“these are their words, not mine”) in formal and written German, especially journalism. Formed from the infinitive stem + endings centred on -e; the key form is 3rd-person singular: er sagt → er sage; sein is irregular: er sei.

Danish trap · no equivalent mood

Danish reports speech with the indicative and at (Han sagde, at situationen var stabil). Konjunktiv I is a genuinely new tool: aim for recognition first (reading newspapers), production later. German reports by mood, not by English-style tense backshift.

29 · Participles as adjectives & extended attributes C1

Formal written German’s signature construction
  • Partizip I (present participle) = infinitive + d: das weinende Kind (the crying child). Active meaning.
  • Partizip II used attributively: das gekaufte Buch (the bought book). Passive / completed meaning.
  • Both decline like adjectives (Ch. 18).
  • Extended participial attributes (erweitertes Partizipialattribut) pack a whole phrase between article and noun: der in der Zeitung veröffentlichte Artikel = “the article published in the newspaper.”
Dansk parallel · unpack into a relative clause

Danish and English forbid stacking such heavy modifiers before the noun and must unpack them: artiklen, som blev offentliggjort i avisen. Reading strategy: find the article, jump to the noun, then read the block in between as a relative clause.

30 · Advanced style C1–C2

Nominalstil, Funktionsverbgefüge, genitive prepositions, subjunctive nuance
  • Nominalstil (nominalisation): formal German turns verbs into nouns: Das Reisen erweitert den Horizont; nach Beendigung des Projekts. Officialese (Amtsdeutsch) is dense with this. Danish also nominalises, but German goes much further; over-nominalising sounds bureaucratic, so use with judgment.
  • Funktionsverbgefüge (light-verb constructions): fixed noun + verb combos where the noun carries the meaning: in Betracht ziehen (take into consideration), zur Verfügung stehen (be available), eine Entscheidung treffen (make a decision), Abschied nehmen (say goodbye). Learn these as whole chunks.
  • Genitive prepositions (formal): während (during), wegen (because of), trotz (despite), (an)statt (instead of), innerhalb / außerhalb: wegen des Wetters, trotz des Regens. In casual speech these increasingly take the dative, but the genitive is the written standard.
  • Subjunctive nuance: at C1/C2, Konjunktiv II carries tone — regret, politeness, diplomatic distance, irony: Das hätte ich nicht gedacht (I would not have thought that); Ich hätte da eine Frage (softened “I have a question”). Mastery is about register, not just form.
  • Compounding: German builds long compound nouns freely — Geschwindigkeitsbegrenzung parallels Danish hastighedsbegrænsning. The compounding instinct transfers directly from Danish; only the linking elements (-s-, -n-) need attention.

Appendices, study plan & caveats

Appendix A · Danish–German sound-shift cheat sheet

High German consonant shift (see Ch. 1):

  • Danish d → German t: kold/kalt, dag/Tag, ord/Wort, rød/rot, dør/Tür
  • Danish t → German z/ss: hjerte/Herz, to/zwei, ti/zehn, salt/Salz
  • Danish k → German ch: mælk/Milch, falsk/falsch, vaske/waschen, seks/sechs
  • Danish sk → German sch: skole/Schule, sko/Schuh, skib/Schiff
  • Danish b/p → German pf/f: æble/Apfel, pibe/Pfeife, hjælpe/helfen, sæbe/Seife

Both languages also prefer k over c: kultur/Kultur, kaffe/Kaffee, musik/Musik.

Study plan for an A2-to-B1 learner

Staged recommendations with thresholds and benchmarks
  1. Weeks 1–2 — Diagnostic & consolidation (A1, Ch. 1–8). Do only the exercises for Ch. 1–8; if you score 90%+, move on. Spend real time only where you drop below 90% — most likely gender (Ch. 2) and nicht/kein (Ch. 8). Threshold to advance: consistent 90% on the exercises.
  2. Weeks 3–8 — Core work (A2–B1, Ch. 9–23). One chapter per 2–3 days: read → quiz → exercise → re-do the exercise from two chapters back (spaced retrieval). Give double time to the three high-interference zones: the four cases (Ch. 9–14), adjective declension (Ch. 18), and verb-final subordinate clauses (Ch. 22). Benchmark: solid B1 when you can produce the Perfekt (Ch. 17) and weil-clauses (Ch. 22) correctly in speech, not just on paper.
  3. Weeks 9–14 — Push to B2 (Ch. 24–27). Passive, Konjunktiv II, zu-infinitives, Plusquamperfekt. Leverage the Danish parallels (werden=blive, zu=at, havde/var) to move fast. Benchmark: you can read a German news article and understand passive and conditional sentences without translating.
  4. Months 4+ — C1/C2 (Ch. 28–30). Treat Konjunktiv I and extended attributes as reading / recognition skills first (newspapers, official letters); produce them only once recognition is automatic. Benchmark for C1: you can unpack an extended participial attribute on sight and use Konjunktiv II for politeness naturally.
  5. Throughout — exploit your Danish, distrust false friends. Use the cognate table (Appendix A) to guess new German words, keep the false-friends list (Appendix B) visible, and when you hit a wall, check whether the German structure has a Danish twin — it usually does.
Signals that should change your plan

Frequent case errors after prepositions → stop and drill Ch. 15 before anything else. Adjective endings feel random → go back to the article grid in Ch. 14. Subordinate-clause verb placement is inconsistent → do daily main-clause / weil-clause transformation drills for a week.

Caveats

  • Grammar level is not the same as CEFR skill level. The chapter tags show where a structure is typically taught; CEFR itself describes communicative ability. You may meet advanced grammar in real input well before you can produce it. Recognition precedes production.
  • The contrastive method has limits. Contrastive analysis in its strong form was criticised from the 1970s for over-predicting errors that do not occur and under-predicting some that do. Use the Danish parallels as scaffolding and error-prevention, not as a guarantee.
  • Gender is only partly rule-governed. The signposts in Ch. 2 have many exceptions; the only reliable method is to learn each noun with its article.
  • Regional and register variation. Southern Germany, Austria and Switzerland use sein as a perfect auxiliary more freely; genitive prepositions often take the dative in speech; the Präteritum/Perfekt split differs by region. The course teaches the written standard.
  • This is a grammar course, not a complete language course. It deliberately omits systematic vocabulary building, listening and speaking practice, and extensive pronunciation drilling — pair it with graded reading, listening, and conversation.
