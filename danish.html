Danish Grammar for German Speakers

Dänische Grammatik für Deutschsprachige — von Anfang bis Fortgeschritten

The mirror of the Danish-to-German course. For a German native, Danish grammar is mostly a story of simplification: no case system, no person endings on verbs, only two genders. The real challenges lie elsewhere: pronunciation (stød, soft d, silent letters), the suffixed definite article, the reflexive possessive sin/sit/sine, adverb placement in subordinate clauses, and the s-passive. Every chapter draws explicit parallels to German so you can move fast where the languages agree and slow down where they diverge.

How to use this course

Methodology · read this first

Why this order?

The spine: sounds → nouns, gender, the suffixed article → pronouns and the one-form present tense → word order → agreement (plurals, adjectives, possessives) → the verb system → complex sentences → the s-passive, particles, and formal style. Unlike German, where the case system dominates everything, Danish front-loads its difficulty into sounds and small structural habits — so this course spends its time differently than its sister volume.

The design follows the same second-language-acquisition research: explicit grammar anchored in meaningful examples (Long’s Focus on Form), graded examples recycling known material (comprehensible-input research: aim to understand 95–98% of each example), quizzes as retrieval practice (Roediger & Karpicke: delayed recall of 61% for a tested group vs. 40% for re-study), and deliberate recycling of earlier grammar (the spacing effect).

How the German parallels help

Wherever Danish works like German, the course says so explicitly (positive transfer) — and that is often: V2 word order, modal verbs, particle verbs, perfect with have/be, compound nouns, even the habit of saying units before tens in numbers. Wherever Danish differs, a German trap box flags the spot where your German instinct produces a Danish error. Grammar terms used: fælleskøn / intetkøn (the two genders), helsætning (main clause), ledsætning (subordinate clause), datid (simple past), førnutid (perfect).

Quiz and exercise routine

After each chapter: quiz (recognition) first, then exercise (production). Answers are hidden behind a button. Then re-do the exercise from two chapters back — spaced retrieval roughly halves forgetting.

Level tags

The A1–C2 tags show where each structure is normally introduced, so you can skip or prioritise. Heads-up: written Danish will feel easy fast; understanding spoken Danish is the long game. Pair this course with listening from day one.

1 · Pronunciation: the real challenge A1

Stød, soft d, silent letters — where all the difficulty lives

Here the tables turn completely. German is nearly phonetic; Danish is not. Written Danish will look almost familiar to you within weeks — spoken Danish is the actual mountain. Budget most of your listening effort here.

  • Three extra letters: æ, ø, å. Map them from German: æ ≈ German ä (læse — “lesen”), ø ≈ German ö (øl — sounds like “Öl” but means beer), å ≈ a long open o, like the vowel in German Sohn but more open (år = Jahr). You can already pronounce all three — positive transfer.
  • The soft d (blødt d): after a vowel, d becomes a sound like a lazy English “th” with the tongue low: mad (food), gade (street), hedde (heißen). German has nothing like it; do not pronounce it as [d].
  • Stød: a creaky glottal catch inside certain syllables, a bit like the catch in German be’achten between e and a — but inside a vowel. It distinguishes word pairs: hun (she, no stød) vs. hund (dog, with stød); læser (reads, stød) vs. læser (reader, no stød). At A1: learn to hear it; production comes later.
  • Silent letters everywhere: final -e is a schwa; d and g often vanish (dag sounds like “dæ”, meget like “majet”); h is silent before v/j (hvad, hjem).
  • r is vocalised after vowels, much like German final -r (Wetter / vejret) — your German habit helps here.
  • y = German ü (ny = neu, sounds like “nü”).
Deutsche Parallele · the sound bridge, reversed

The High German consonant shift means your German predicts Danish spellings by shifting backwards: German t → Danish d (Tag/dag), German z/ss → Danish t (zwei/to, Wasser/vand), German ch → Danish k (Milch/mælk), German sch → Danish sk (Schule/skole), German pf/f → Danish b/p (Apfel/æble, helfen/hjælpe). Full table in Appendix A.

2 · Nouns & two genders A1

Your three genders collapse into two — but not predictably

Good news first: Danish nouns are not capitalised (except names), and there are only two genders:

Danish genderArticleShareCorresponds to German
fælleskøn (common, “n-words”)enabout 75%der + die merged
intetkøn (neuter, “t-words”)etabout 25%roughly das
German trap · do not project your der/die/das

German gender does not reliably predict Danish gender: das Haus → et hus works, but das Kind → et barn while die Zeit → en tid and der Löffel → en ske. Since ~75% of nouns are n-words, when in doubt guess en — but learn each noun with its article, exactly as you would tell a Dane learning German.

Useful signposts: living beings are usually en (en mand, en kvinde, en hund — but et barn, et dyr); substances and many abstract categories lean et (et år, et sprog). Suffix parallels transfer nicely: German -heit/-keit → Danish -hed (Freiheit/frihed, both common gender), German -ung → Danish -(n)ing (Regierung/regering, both common gender).

3 · The suffixed definite article A1

huset, not “det hus” — the mirror of the German trap

This is the structural surprise for a German. Danish attaches “the” as a suffix:

indefinitedefiniteGerman
commonen mandmandender Mann
neuteret hushusetdas Haus
pluralbilerbilernedie Autos
German trap · the article switches sides with adjectives

As soon as an adjective is added, the suffix disappears and a free-standing article appears in front, German-style: den store bil, det store hus, de store biler. Never both: den store bilen is wrong in Danish (that is Swedish/Norwegian “double definiteness”). Rule of thumb: no adjective → suffix; adjective → den/det/de in front.

Deutsche Parallele

The indefinite articles map directly: ein/eine ≈ en/et. And den/det/de before adjectives behaves exactly like your der/die/das — a free-standing definite article. The only new habit is the suffix in the plain case.

4 · Pronouns & the present tense A1

One verb form for everyone — enjoy the liberation

Personal pronouns (subject): jeg (ich), du (du), han / hun / den / det (er / sie / es — den for common-gender things, det for neuter things), vi (wir), I (ihr — note the capital I!), de (sie/pl), De (formal Sie, today archaic).

Present tense: infinitive + -r, one form for all persons:

DanishGerman
jeg spiller, du spiller, han spiller, vi spiller, I spiller, de spillerich spiele, du spielst, er spielt, wir spielen, ihr spielt, sie spielen
Deutsche Parallele · one present tense

Like German, Danish has no progressive: jeg spiller = “ich spiele” and “ich spiele gerade”. Your instinct transfers directly.

German trap · du vs. De

Modern Danish uses du with practically everyone — strangers, bosses, officials. The formal De survives only in very formal letters and with the royal family. Suppress your Sie-reflex: saying De to a shop assistant sounds stiff, not polite. Also: never conjugate for person — du spillerst does not exist.

6 · Word order: the V2 rule A1

Your biggest head start — identical in both languages

Danish main clauses (helsætninger) obey the same verb-second rule as German: finite verb second, one constituent in front, inversion when something else is fronted.

  • Jeg drikker kaffe i dag. / Ich trinke heute Kaffee.
  • I dag drikker jeg kaffe. / Heute trinke ich Kaffee. — identical inversion.
German trap · the bracket is weaker

German loves the Satzklammer with the infinitive at the very end after long middles. Danish also sends non-finite verbs toward the end (jeg vil gerne spise = ich möchte gern essen), but the middle field is shorter and objects normally follow the verb cluster more tightly. Danish also never does verb-final subordinate clauses — see Ch. 21, the biggest word-order difference for you.

7 · Questions A1

Inversion and hv-words — your w-words in disguise
  • Yes/no questions: verb first, exactly like German: Drikker du kaffe? = Trinkst du Kaffee?
  • Hv-questions: Danish question words start with hv- (your w-): hvem (wer), hvad (was), hvor (wo), hvornår (wann), hvorfor (warum), hvordan (wie), hvilken (welcher). The h is silent: hvad sounds like “vað”.

8 · Negation with ikke A1

One negator instead of two — nicht and kein merge

Danish uses one word, ikke, for nearly everything German splits into nicht and kein:

  • Jeg arbejder ikke. = Ich arbeite nicht.
  • Jeg har ikke en bil / more idiomatic: Jeg har ingen bil. = Ich habe kein Auto. (ingen/intet = kein, optional but common.)
  • Placement in main clauses: after the finite verb (and after object pronouns): Jeg kender ham ikke = Ich kenne ihn nicht.
German trap · ikke moves in ledsætninger

In subordinate clauses, ikke jumps before the finite verb: …fordi jeg ikke arbejder (…weil ich nicht arbeite). This placement shift is the single most persistent Danish word-order error for foreigners — preview here, full treatment in Ch. 21.

9 · Where did the cases go? A1–A2

No Akkusativ, no Dativ — word order and prepositions do the work

Danish has essentially abolished the case system on nouns and articles. Manden ser hunden — subject and object look identical; only word order tells you who does what (like English). No preposition governs a case: med manden, til manden, for manden — the noun never changes.

  • Pronouns keep a two-way case distinction (subject vs. object), like your er/ihn: jeg/mig, du/dig, han/ham, hun/hende, den/den, det/det, vi/os, I/jer, de/dem. There is no dative: ham covers both ihn and ihm.
  • Genitive: a simple -s on anything, no article change: mandens bil = das Auto des Mannes. Even whole phrases: kongen af Danmarks slot (the King of Denmark’s castle). Alternative with preposition: bilen til manden.
German trap · stop declining

Your instinct to mark den/dem will produce non-existent forms. There is no *mandem, no case after prepositions, no adjective case endings. The flip side: because case no longer disambiguates, Danish word order is less free than German — fronting an object (Manden ser hunden vs. Hunden ser manden) changes the meaning.

11 · Adjective agreement A2

Only three forms — but they also apply after “to be”

Danish adjectives have three forms — no case dimension at all:

FormUsed withExample
basecommon gender singularen stor bil · bilen er stor
-tneuter singularet stort hus · huset er stort
-eall plurals and all definite formsstore biler · den store bil · min store bil
German trap · predicative adjectives agree!

In German, adjectives after sein are uninflected: das Haus ist groß. In Danish they agree: huset er stort, bilerne er store. Forgetting the -t/-e after “er” is a classic German-speaker error. Second trap: after a possessive or genitive, the definite -e form appears even without den/det/de: min store bil, Kristines gamle hus.

Deutsche Parallele

The attributive system is a radically simplified version of yours: think of -t as the neuter marker (like ein großes Haus → et stort hus) and -e as the weak ending after definite articles (der große Mann → den store mand). Same logic, three forms instead of dozens.

12 · Possessives & sin / sit / sine A2–B1

The reflexive possessive German lost — the classic trap

Possessives: min/mit/mine (mein), din/dit/dine (dein), hans (sein — his), hendes (ihr — her), dens/dets (its), vores (unser), jeres (euer), deres (ihr/pl). min/mit/mine agree like adjectives (common/neuter/plural); the -s forms never change.

German trap · sin, sit, sine

Danish has a reflexive 3rd-person possessive that German lacks. When the possessor is the subject of the same clause, you must use sin/sit/sine; hans/hendes then means someone else’s:

Han tager sin bil. = Er nimmt sein eigenes Auto.
Han tager hans bil. = Er nimmt dessen Auto (das eines anderen Mannes).

German sein covers both, so you must learn to ask: same subject? → sin/sit/sine. This distinction is meaning-changing and Danes notice the error immediately.

1–20 are familiar cousins: en/et, to, tre, fire, fem, seks, syv, otte, ni, ti, elleve, tolv, tretten … tyve. Then: tredive (30), fyrre (40) — and from 50, Danish counts in twenties:

14 · Prepositions A2–B1

No case to govern — but i, på and til follow their own logic

Danish prepositions never change the noun. The difficulty is idiomatic choice, especially i vs. på:

  • i (in): i Berlin, i huset, i januar — but also i skole, i biografen (institutions).
  • på (auf/an): på bordet (auf dem Tisch), but idiomatically på arbejde (auf der Arbeit), på hotel, på Fyn (islands!), på universitetet.
  • til (zu/nach + destination, and possession): jeg tager til København (ich fahre nach Kopenhagen), en gave til dig.
  • hos = French “chez”, German bei: hos lægen (beim Arzt), hos os (bei uns).
  • om (about; also “in” for future time): om to timer (in zwei Stunden) — vs. for to timer siden (vor zwei Stunden).
  • Movement vs. location is expressed with adverb pairs, not case: ind i / inde i (hinein/drinnen), op / oppe, ud / ude, hen / henne. Compare your wohin/wo distinction — the concept is identical, the mechanism is adverb form instead of Akkusativ/Dativ.
German trap

Do not translate nach blindly: destinations take til (til Danmark), never *nach. And islands take på: på Sjælland, på Bornholm.

16 · Particle verbs A2–B1

Your trennbare Verben, Danish style — stress decides meaning

Danish partikelverber work like your separable verbs: at stå op (aufstehen): Jeg står op klokken 7 = Ich stehe um 7 Uhr auf. The particle goes toward the end of the clause — a completely familiar mechanism.

  • Common pairs: stå op (aufstehen), tage af sted (losfahren), holde op (aufhören), finde ud af (herausfinden), se ud (aussehen), gå ud (ausgehen).

17 · Past tenses: datid & førnutid A2–B1

-ede/-te and har/er — but the register split runs the other way

Datid (simple past): weak verbs add -ede or -te: spillede (spielte), læste (las); strong verbs change the vowel: gik (ging), så (sah), kom (kam), drak (trank) — often the same ablaut families as German.

Førnutid (perfect): har/er + participle (-et/-t): Jeg har spillet fodbold = Ich habe Fußball gespielt. Motion and change-of-state verbs take er, exactly like your sein-verbs: Jeg er kørt til Berlin = Ich bin nach Berlin gefahren. The haben/sein split maps almost one-to-one onto have/være.

German trap · speak in datid

In spoken German you say ich habe gespielt; Präteritum sounds literary. Danish is the opposite of your habit: the simple past (jeg spillede) is completely normal in speech for completed past events, while førnutid is reserved for present relevance (like English). Saying jeg har spillet fodbold i går is off; with a past time adverbial use datid: jeg spillede fodbold i går.

19 · Talking about the future B1

Present + time word, vil, skal — no dedicated future tense

21 · Ledsætninger: adverb placement B1

The big word-order difference — the verb does NOT go to the end

Here your German instinct betrays you most. Danish subordinate clauses (ledsætninger, after at, fordi, hvis, når, om, selvom, mens) do not send the verb to the end. Instead, the rule is: Subject – sentence adverb – finite verb:

Main clauseSubordinate clause
DanishJeg kommer ikke.…fordi jeg ikke kommer.
GermanIch komme nicht.…weil ich nicht komme. (verb final)

So the diagnostic word is ikke (and its siblings altid, aldrig, også, nok, jo): main clause → after the verb; ledsætning → before the verb. Objects and complements follow the verb as usual — Danish never piles them up before it.

German trap · resist verb-final

Producing *fordi jeg kommer ikke (main-clause order) or trying to build German-style verb-final chains are the two classic errors. Drill the pattern: fordi jeg ikke kommer, at hun altid arbejder, hvis du ikke vil. Coordinating conjunctions (og, men, eller, for) keep main-clause order — same as your und/aber/oder/denn.

Fronted subordinate clauses trigger inversion in the main clause, exactly like German: Hvis det regner, bliver vi hjemme = Wenn es regnet, bleiben wir zu Hause.

22 · Relative clauses: der & som B1

Two little uninflected words replace your whole der/den/dem paradigm
  • som works for subject or object: manden, som jeg ser (der Mann, den ich sehe).
  • der works only as subject: manden, der står der (der Mann, der dort steht). As subject, der and som are interchangeable.
  • As an object, the pronoun can be dropped entirely, like English: manden, jeg ser — impossible in German, completely normal in Danish.
  • No case, no gender, no number on the relative pronoun. Possessive relative (“dessen/deren”) = hvis: manden, hvis bil…
  • Word order inside the relative clause is ledsætning order (Ch. 21): manden, som ikke kommer.
German trap

Do not hunt for a case-marked pronoun — there is none. And when the relative pronoun is the subject, you cannot drop it (manden, der står der — der/som obligatory); dropping is only allowed for objects.

24 · The two passives B2

blive-passive (familiar) and the s-passive (new)
  • blive-passive: blive + participle — a perfect mirror of your werden-passive: Brevet bliver skrevet = Der Brief wird geschrieben. Tends to describe single, concrete events.
  • s-passive: add -s to the verb — no auxiliary at all: Brevet skrives. Tends to describe habitual, general or normative statements: Døren åbnes klokken 8 (Die Tür wird um 8 geöffnet — regelmäßig), dansk tales i Danmark. Very common after modals: Det kan ses (das kann man sehen / das ist zu sehen).
  • State passive: være + participle, like your sein-Passiv: Døren er åbnet = Die Tür ist geöffnet.
  • Agent with af (= von): Bogen blev skrevet af en dansker.
  • Bonus: some verbs exist only in s-form with non-passive meaning (deponent verbs): findes (existieren — der findes mange muligheder), synes (finden/meinen), mødes (sich treffen), lykkes (gelingen).
German trap

The s-passive has no German counterpart — do not try to force werden into every passive. Rule of thumb: specific event → blive; general rule, habit, instruction, or after a modal → -s. In formal writing the s-passive dominates.

25 · Conditionals & hypotheticals B2

No Konjunktiv — datid does the job

Danish has no subjunctive mood. Where German reaches for Konjunktiv II, Danish uses past-tense forms plus ville:

  • Hvis jeg havde tid, ville jeg læse mere. = Wenn ich Zeit hätte, würde ich mehr lesen. Structure identical; Danish just uses plain havde instead of hätte.
  • Hvis jeg var rig… = Wenn ich reich wäre…
  • Past hypotheticals: Hvis jeg havde vidst det, ville jeg være blevet hjemme / colloquially var jeg blevet hjemme. = …wäre ich zu Hause geblieben.
  • Politeness: ville and kunne soften requests exactly like würde/könnte: Kunne du hjælpe mig? = Könntest du mir helfen? Jeg ville gerne have en kaffe = Ich hätte gern einen Kaffee.
Deutsche Parallele

Because German Konjunktiv II forms (hätte, wäre, könnte) are historically just umlauted past forms, the Danish system will feel like German with the umlauts removed: havde ≈ hätte, var ≈ wäre, kunne ≈ könnte, ville ≈ würde. The mapping is nearly mechanical.

28 · Reported speech C1

No Konjunktiv I — tense shift and word order do the work

German marks reported speech with a mood (Konjunktiv I: er sei krank). Danish has no such mood; instead:

  • Use at + indicative, usually with tense backshift after a past reporting verb (like English): Han sagde, at han var syg = Er sagte, er sei krank.
  • Remember ledsætning word order in the at-clause: Han sagde, at han ikke kom.
  • Reported questions: om (= ob): Hun spurgte, om jeg kom; hv-questions keep the hv-word: Hun spurgte, hvornår jeg kom. Note: when the hv-word is the subject, insert der: Hun spurgte, hvem der kom.
  • Commands: at + skulle: Han sagde, at jeg skulle vente (er sagte, ich solle warten).
German trap

Do not import Konjunktiv: there is no Danish “sei/hätten” equivalent, and inventing one is impossible — the temptation is rather to forget the backshift or the ikke-placement. Drill: reporting verb in past → shift the clause verb one step back; adverb before the verb.

29 · Discourse particles: jo, da, nok, vel C1

Denmark’s Modalpartikeln — your secret German superpower

Danish, like German, seasons every sentence with small untranslatable particles — the famous doch/ja/wohl/mal/schon game. Most learners suffer here; you already think in Modalpartikeln, so map them:

DanishGerman feelExample
jodoch / ja (shared knowledge)Det ved du jo. (Das weißt du ja/doch.)
dadoch (insistence, contrast)Kom da ind! (Komm doch rein!)
nokwohl / schon (assumption, reassurance)Det går nok. (Das wird schon.)
velwohl / oder? (seeking agreement)Du kommer vel? (Du kommst doch, oder?)
vistwohl (hearsay/uncertainty)Han er vist syg. (Er ist wohl krank.)
ligemal / ebenKan du lige hjælpe? (Kannst du mal helfen?)
godtschon / durchausJeg kan godt lide det. (Ich mag es durchaus / ich mag es.)

Also jo as an answer = your doch after negative questions: Kommer du ikke? — Jo! (Kommst du nicht? — Doch!). A perfect one-to-one match English speakers can only envy.

30 · Advanced style C1–C2

Compounds, formal Danish, adverb formation, and idiomatic polish
  • Compounding: Danish builds compounds exactly like German, with linking -s- and -e-: hastighedsbegrænsning = Geschwindigkeitsbegrenzung, arbejdsmarked = Arbeitsmarkt. Writing compounds as two words (*hastigheds begrænsning) is a real orthographic error in Danish too — your German one-word instinct is correct.
  • Adverbs from adjectives: add -t: han kører hurtigt (er fährt schnell). German leaves adjectives bare as adverbs; Danish marks them — forgetting the -t is a giveaway German accent in writing.
  • Formal/written style: official Danish favours the s-passive (Ch. 24), nominalisations in -else, -ning, -hed (beslutning = Entscheidung, undersøgelse = Untersuchung), and light-verb chunks: træffe en beslutning (eine Entscheidung treffen — the same idiom!), stå til rådighed (zur Verfügung stehen), tage afsked (Abschied nehmen). Amtssprache habits transfer almost embarrassingly well.
  • “man” exists and works like yours: man kan ikke vide det = man kann es nicht wissen. Object form: en; possessive: ens.
  • Register: spoken Danish contracts aggressively (det er → det’, ikke → ik’); written Danish stays close to what this course teaches. Expect the gap between the two to be far larger than in German.

Appendices, study plan & caveats

Appendix A · German–Danish sound-shift cheat sheet

Shift your German backwards through the High German consonant shift to guess Danish words:

  • German t → Danish d: kalt/kold, Tag/dag, Wort/ord, rot/rød, Tür/dør
  • German z/ss → Danish t: Herz/hjerte, zwei/to, zehn/ti, Salz/salt, Wasser/vand
  • German ch → Danish k: Milch/mælk, falsch/falsk, waschen/vaske, sechs/seks
  • German sch → Danish sk: Schule/skole, Schuh/sko, Schiff/skib
  • German pf/f → Danish b/p: Apfel/æble, Pfeife/pibe, helfen/hjælpe, Seife/sæbe

Both languages prefer k over c: Kultur/kultur, Kaffee/kaffe, Musik/musik.

Study plan for a German-speaking beginner

Staged recommendations with thresholds and benchmarks
  1. Weeks 1–2 — Foundations (Ch. 1–8). Grammar-wise these are fast for you; spend the saved time on Chapter 1 and raw listening (slow Danish podcasts, DR with subtitles). Threshold: you can read simple sentences aloud with soft d and roughly correct vowels, and score 90% on the exercises.
  2. Weeks 3–8 — Core (Ch. 9–23). One chapter per 2–3 days: read → quiz → exercise → re-do the exercise from two chapters back. Give double time to the four German-trap zones: predicative adjective agreement (Ch. 11), sin/sit/sine (Ch. 12), må/skal (Ch. 15), and adverb placement in ledsætninger (Ch. 21). Benchmark: you can say fordi-sentences with correct ikke-placement in speech without pausing.
  3. Weeks 9–14 — Push on (Ch. 24–27). The s-passive is the one genuinely new system; the conditionals and pluskvamperfektum are nearly free for a German. Benchmark: you can read a DR news article and correctly parse every -s verb form as passive, deponent, or genitive.
  4. Months 4+ — Polish (Ch. 28–30). Reported speech, particles, style. The particle chapter (29) is your unfair advantage — drill it early if you enjoy it; nothing makes you sound more native faster. Benchmark: you can use jo, nok, vel and lige naturally.
  5. Throughout — listening above all. Written Danish will surrender quickly to a German speaker; spoken Danish will not. Make listening a daily habit from week 1, use the sound-shift table (Appendix A) to guess vocabulary, and keep the false-friends list (Appendix B) visible.
Signals that should change your plan

If Danes switch to English when you speak: work on prosody and the soft d, not grammar. If you keep saying “må” for müssen: re-drill Ch. 15 immediately — it is meaning-changing. If your ikke keeps landing after the verb in fordi-clauses: do daily transformation drills (main clause → ledsætning) for a week.

Caveats

  • Grammar level is not the same as CEFR skill level. The tags show where a structure is typically taught. In Danish specifically, listening comprehension will lag your grammar level by a wide margin — that is normal and expected.
  • The contrastive method has limits. Contrastive analysis over-predicts some errors and misses others; use the German parallels as scaffolding, not a guarantee. Some Danish difficulties (stød, vowel inventory) have nothing to do with German interference.
  • Gender is only partly rule-governed. The en/et assignment must be memorised per noun; German gender is not a reliable predictor.
  • Register and regional variation. Spoken Danish reduces heavily and varies (Jutland vs. Copenhagen); the course teaches the written standard (rigsdansk). The du/De guidance reflects mainstream usage; a few formal contexts still use De.
  • This is a grammar course, not a complete language course. It deliberately omits systematic vocabulary building and — critically for Danish — extensive pronunciation and listening training. Pair it with daily audio input from day one.
